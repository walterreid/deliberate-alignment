**CHAPTER FOUR**

# **Deliberate Alignment**

*The room is not where the work gets reported. It is where the work begins.*

There is a name for what happens in the best two and a half hours of the Thursday meeting.

Not a new name. The thing it describes has been happening in good rooms for as long as there have been good rooms. What is new is the urgency of naming it — the specific pressure that arrives when execution becomes fast enough that the thing which was always most important can no longer be deferred past the first conversation.

Deliberate Alignment is a methodology organized around the quality of the decision before execution begins, rather than the management of execution itself. The unit of work is not the sprint. It is the room — the specific convergence of the right people, with visible work, making real decisions that an agent can act on before anyone leaves.

That definition is precise. It is also incomplete without understanding what it is not.

It is not better meetings. Meetings, as they exist in most organizations, are designed either to communicate decisions already made elsewhere or to create the appearance of collective ownership for decisions that will be made elsewhere anyway. The calendar entry and the agenda and the agreed-upon next steps are theater when the real decision has already happened in a hallway or a direct message. Deliberate Alignment is not about running that process more efficiently. It is a different process.

It is not a workshop methodology. Design sprints, war rooms, hackathons — these are relatives of the same instinct, and the iHeart story shows where that instinct comes from. The hackathon changed what was being decided on. A tangible prototype reduces decision latency by changing what the process has to evaluate. Deliberate Alignment takes that instinct and makes it the entire methodology rather than a periodic intervention.

And it is not a framework for eliminating disagreement. The room that produces genuine alignment is not a room where everyone agrees. It is a room where the disagreement is legible — where the tension between perspectives is visible to everyone, where it can be evaluated rather than avoided, and where someone with the authority to commit can make the call and have it mean something. Alignment is not consensus. It is shared understanding of a direction, including an understanding of what was decided against and why.

## **The Three Functions**

Every room that has ever produced genuine alignment has contained three functions. Sometimes three people, sometimes fewer. The number of people is not the point. The functions are.

The first is the function of depth. Someone in the room has to understand what the client, the user, the stakeholder needs beneath what they say. Not the stated requirement. The actual pressure. The competitive anxiety that didn't make it into the brief. The internal politics that will determine whether the right solution gets adopted or quietly shelved.

In the Zansei project, this function belongs to the person who understands that Zan's requests for new features are not really product requests. They are expressions of a founder's anxiety about whether the thing he has built will matter. When he says "now let's add e-commerce," he is not describing a product roadmap. He is describing fear that the current product is not enough. The person who hears that and responds to the fear rather than the feature request is doing the depth function. The person who adds e-commerce to the backlog is not.

This function has been systematically undervalued because it is invisible when it is working. The meeting goes smoothly. The client leaves satisfied. The direction is clear. Nobody sees the specific quality of listening that produced those outcomes because the outcomes look like they just happened.

The second is the function of taste. Someone in the room has to be willing to say when the answer is defaulting to mediocrity. Not the obvious wrong answer — that is easy to catch. The answer that is technically correct, defensible, and insufficient. The direction that will satisfy the sprint review and disappoint the user.

This function is the most politically exposed of the three because it requires pushing back against the path of least resistance in a room where the path of least resistance is what everyone wants to take. It requires a specific kind of standing — not authority, exactly, but credibility. The person who says "that's not good enough" has to have earned the right to say it. Which means the function cannot be assigned. It can only be recognized.

In the Zansei project, this function is currently vacant. Not because the people in the room lack taste — several of them have excellent taste. But nobody has been given, or taken, the explicit role of maintaining the product's integrity against the gravity of scope expansion. When Zan expands the scope, there is no voice in the room whose designated function is to say "that's a different product and building it will cost us the product we have." That absence is structural, not personal.

The third is the function of authority. Someone in the room has to be able to say yes and have it mean yes. Not yes pending approval. Not yes subject to review. Yes, this is the direction, and the execution can begin.

This function sounds simple. It is the most complicated of the three in practice, because authority without understanding is not authority — it is permission for the wrong thing. The person who can commit must be present in the room where the alignment is being built, not receiving a summary afterward. The summary degrades the alignment. It removes the context that made the direction legible and replaces it with a conclusion that looks like the same thing but isn't.

Zan is the authority function in the Zansei project. The Thursday meeting works, when it works, partly because he is there — present, engaged, making calls in real time. The meetings where he is absent or distracted produce decisions that look like decisions until he encounters them later and reframes them from a different context. The alignment wasn't built with him. It was built around him. Those are not the same thing.

Understanding Zan requires accepting something the framework does not make obvious: authority without depth is not always a vacancy to be filled. Sometimes it is the permanent operating condition of the person who holds the authority function. Zan is the type of person who tells you to go take pictures in Central Park. And when someone asks which part — the geese, the lake, the people, the trees — he says "Central Park." Or worse: "I told you Central Park, what don't you understand." He does not know what he wants until he sees options. He has never known. He is not going to start.

This type of person thrives with AI, assuming there are people who can translate those words to actions. Before, you had to go photograph everything in Central Park and return like an episode of Mad Men, lay the options on his desk, and have him select. Now the options can be generated in minutes. The translation layer — the people who hear "Central Park" and understand what questions to ask before anyone starts shooting — is more important, not less. The framework's job is not to fix the founder. It is to build the translation layer that lets the founder's instinct become actionable without requiring the founder to articulate what he cannot articulate.

The danger is what happens when Zan sees the speed. If you can provide a full prototype in a week that would have taken months before, he will expect the mobile app or the multi-tenant system done by next Monday. Expectation inflation — the authority function seeing AI-enabled speed on one task and generalizing it to everything — is a failure mode the framework has to absorb. The translation layer has to manage not just the founder's direction but the founder's sense of what is possible.

## **Why One Person Can Hold Two Functions**

The three functions are not job descriptions. They are capacities that need to be present in the room. The same person can hold more than one.

The person who understands what the client needs beneath what they say often also has the taste to recognize when the answer is insufficient. Those two functions travel together in people who have spent significant time close to users — who have developed their taste through repeated exposure to the gap between what was built and what was needed.

The person with authority to commit sometimes also holds the depth function — the founder who has been closest to the problem, who understands the user's anxiety because they shared it before they built the solution. That combination is powerful when the authority is paired with genuine understanding and dangerous when it isn't. Zan at his best is both. Zan on a day when he is running on pattern-matching from a single data point is the authority function operating without the depth function, and the direction that emerges will be confident and wrong.

What the framework requires is not that three distinct people occupy three distinct boxes. It requires that when any significant decision is being made, all three functions are present and active. The check is simple: is someone listening at depth, is someone willing to say when it isn't good enough, and is someone able to commit with genuine understanding of what they are committing to? If any of those questions has no answer, the room is not ready to produce alignment.

The framework requires that the functions be present. It does not require that they remain distributed across multiple people indefinitely.

## **The New Prioritization Question**

The old prioritization question — given scarce execution capacity, what should we build first? — dissolved in the previous chapter when the denominator disappeared. The new question is: given that we can build almost anything, what should we decide to build at all?

That is not a ranking problem. It is a decision problem. And it requires the three functions to be present. The depth function to understand what building this will actually mean for the person it is built for. The taste function to assess whether this is the direction that will matter or merely the direction that is available. The authority function to commit to one direction and foreclose the others.

The decision that resolves ambiguity for every execution that follows it is worth more than the decision that resolves one task cleanly. The decision that lets the room disperse with shared understanding of what the next month looks like is worth more than the decision that produces one feature quickly.

This is what the Zansei team is missing. Not execution capacity — the team can build almost anything in hours. What it is missing is the decision that would let every member build confidently in the same direction without requiring constant re-alignment. The Thursday meeting produces that decision, sometimes, for a few days. The methodology is the attempt to produce it reliably.

## **What the Room Requires**

A room that produces genuine alignment requires that the work is visible. Not described — visible. The prototype, the draft, the live system, the actual artifact that the decision is being made about. The client who is reacting to a description is imagining something. The client who is reacting to a prototype is evaluating something. These produce different quality decisions.

It requires that the people in the room can make the decision. Not advise on it. Make it, with authority that holds outside the room. A room full of people who are present to inform a decision that will be made by someone who is not present is not an alignment session. It is a research process that will produce a summary that will degrade the alignment before it reaches the person who can commit.

It requires that the decision survives the room. The output of a Deliberate Alignment session is not minutes. It is a shared understanding explicit enough to be acted on independently — specific enough that two people who were in the room can make separate decisions the following day that point in the same direction without checking with each other first.

And it requires that the people in the room trust each other enough to say what they actually think. Psychological safety — the condition where people believe they will not be punished for speaking up — is not a soft organizational concern in this context. It is load-bearing. The room that cannot hold genuine disagreement cannot produce genuine alignment. What it produces instead is the appearance of alignment, which is more dangerous than acknowledged disagreement because it is invisible until the execution is already underway in the wrong direction.

## **The Framework Applied to Zansei**

The Zansei project is not a success story. It is a work in progress, and the framework is being tested on it in real time.

What the Thursday meeting gets right: it assembles most of the functions. The depth is present. The authority is present when Zan engages rather than absorbs. The work is visible on the screen at the end of the table. For two and a half hours, the room functions as the methodology describes.

There was a Thursday in March where it worked. The business consultant, who held the client relationship, asked the developer to produce a brand document and a white-label overview — something a new partner could use to get up to speed quickly. The request was reasonable. The timeline she was imagining was a week. The UX lead, who could have built it with her eyes closed, sat quietly. The developer nodded.

I was annoyed before I understood why I was annoyed. The business consultant had been in every meeting — she was the person closest to the partner's needs. The UX lead was sitting right next to her, the person who could articulate the brand's voice in her sleep. And both of them had deferred the ask to the developer. Not because he knew the brand better. Because producing a deliverable still felt like a technical act, even when the deliverable was pure judgment. The knowledge to produce those documents was in the room. It had been in the room for months. The taste and the depth were present. They just didn't recognize themselves as sufficient.

So I did it while the conversation continued. Opened Claude, shared screenshots of the product flow, attached two media briefs as examples, and asked for both deliverables. Before the meeting moved to the next topic, both documents were in Slack. Not perfect. Good enough to use, good enough to improve, produced in five minutes from the understanding that was already present in the room.

The room went quiet for a moment. Not because the documents were impressive — they were functional, not beautiful. Because the speed made visible something the team had not confronted directly: the gap between what they knew collectively and what they were producing was not a capacity problem. It was a willingness problem. The understanding was there. The encoding was not.

And then the thing I did not say out loud, because it was not the moment: I could produce those documents because I held all three functions simultaneously — I understood what the partner needed, I could judge whether the output was sufficient, and I had the authority to ship it without asking. The business consultant had the depth. The UX lead had the taste. Both were in the room. Neither acted. So I held all three, and for five minutes the framework worked perfectly — and the question I could not stop thinking afterward was why I had needed anyone else in the room at all. That question is not one this chapter can answer. But it is one the book will return to.


What it gets wrong: the taste function is not held consistently. The decision that the meeting produces is not always encoded explicitly enough to survive the week. The authority that commits on Thursday sometimes re-opens what was committed on the following Monday, having encountered a new piece of information that was not in the room. The alignment is real but fragile — produced by proximity and presence, not by a system designed to sustain it. Initially, the Thursdays were demos without direction — work shown, reactions given, nothing decided that would hold past the weekend.

The fix is not to have longer meetings or more meetings. It is to be more deliberate about what the meeting is for. The first fifteen minutes of ritual — what did you do, what will you do, any blockers — is not alignment work. It is status reporting that could be asynchronous. The two and a half hours in the middle is the alignment work.

The more fundamental fix is to name the vacancy. The taste function is not held. Nobody in the room has the explicit standing to say "that is a different product" when the scope expands, and have the authority function hear it as information rather than resistance. Without that voice, the backlog of features and client requests becomes what the previous chapter called an anxiety archive — not a prioritization tool but a growing list of things that might matter, maintained by a team that cannot say no. Naming that vacancy is not a personnel problem. It requires a conversation about what the room needs that it does not currently have, and who is willing to hold it.


That conversation is itself a Deliberate Alignment session. The methodology is recursive. The room that designs the room is the same kind of room as the room it is designing.

The Zansei project will continue after this book is written. Some Thursdays will produce the two and a half hours of real alignment. Some will produce demos without direction.

What the framework offers is not a guarantee that every room will work. It is a description of what a room that works requires, specific enough that you can look at your own room and ask which of those things are present and which are absent. The three functions. The visible work. The decision that survives the room. The trust that makes genuine disagreement possible.

If all of those are present, something will happen in the room that is difficult to describe from the outside and immediately recognizable from the inside. The options become real. The direction becomes clear. The people leave knowing what they are building and why, and the knowledge is specific enough to act on without checking back.

That is what the Thursday meeting feels like in its best two and a half hours. Not efficient. Not optimized. Alive.

It is not a meeting. It is the work.

*The room that produces alignment is not the room where everyone agrees. It is the room where the decision is made by the people who have to live with it, in the presence of everything they need to make it well.*
