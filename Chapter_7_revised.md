# CHAPTER SEVEN
# Relief or Panic — How to Know Which You Are

*This chapter is addressed to you. Not your organization. You.*

Every other chapter in this book talks about organizations, systems, teams, and industries. It talks about bottlenecks and constraints and the structural logic of why methodology changes when economics change.

This chapter is different.

This chapter asks you to stop reading about the shift and start locating yourself inside it. Because the shift is not something that happens to your company and then, separately, to you. It is something that will happen to you in a way that your job title, your industry, and your methodology will not adequately prepare you for.

The question this chapter is built around is simple. When execution becomes cheap and the bottleneck moves to decision quality, do you experience that as relief or as panic?

The honest answer to that question tells you more about your situation than any organizational diagnostic. And most people, if they are being truthful, will not know their answer until the moment arrives.

This chapter is an attempt to help you know before that.

## The Cover You Did Not Know You Had

There is a thing that slow systems do that nobody talks about, because it only becomes visible when the slowness goes away.

They carry you.

Not in the way a safety net carries you. More quietly than that. The process absorbs a portion of the judgment that should have been yours. The sprint planning meeting distributes the decision-making across enough voices that no single person has to be certain. The handoff from designer to developer to QA creates enough distance that by the time something ships, the original intention has been interpreted and reinterpreted enough times that nobody is entirely accountable for the final result. The timeline gives everyone cover. We ran out of time. The requirements changed. We need another sprint.

This is not cynicism. These dynamics are real and they exist in good teams, not just dysfunctional ones. Friction was never just inefficiency. It was also a buffer — a shared processing period, a political equalizer that gave everyone in the room roughly the same amount of time to figure out what they thought before they had to say it out loud.

When the friction disappears, the cover goes with it.

What is left is the judgment. Yours. Unmediated. Immediate. Visible.

For some people, this is the moment they have been waiting for without knowing they were waiting for it. The process was always getting in the way. They had the judgment. They just kept having to route it through twelve other people and wait two weeks for it to emerge on the other side, diluted.

For others, this is the moment they realize, with a specific quality of stomach-drop, that the process was doing more work for them than they knew.

Both of these people are sitting in the same meeting. Both of them are watching the same demo of what the agent built overnight. One of them is relieved. One of them is afraid. And neither of them can fully explain why they feel what they feel, because the thing that changed is invisible.

## The End of Borrowed Identity

Your job title has been doing something for you that you probably have not examined.

It has been telling you and everyone around you what you are good at, what you are for, and where you fit. Not because titles are accurate — they rarely are — but because in a world of specialized roles, the title was the closest available proxy for capability. You were a developer. That meant something. It placed you in a system with legible inputs and outputs, with a defined relationship to the designer upstream and the QA person downstream, with a body of craft that took years to develop and that other people could not simply decide to do tomorrow.

That proxy is breaking.

When a designer can build the product, and a business developer can build the product, and a product manager can build the product, and each of them can do it in a morning using the same tools — the title stops functioning as a reliable signal of what a person brings that someone else cannot. The specialization that the title represented either deepens into something genuinely irreplaceable, or it becomes a credential for a capability that is no longer scarce.

There is a version of this that is already happening and that no one has named adequately. A team member on a project I was part of built a video generation tool — marketing briefs that turned into thirty-second videos, production quality that would have required a studio and a team five years ago. He built it with AI tools and his own experience on real production sets. It was genuinely impressive. And nobody needed it. Building something you couldn't have dreamed of five years ago and having no one care is crushing. Five years ago the idea would have been pressure-tested by twenty-five people and refined, but now there isn't connection because development is so fast there isn't time to connect before it's done. The new capability outpaced any framework for valuing it. The title — producer, creative director, whatever it was — no longer described what he could do, and what he could do had no place to land.

The first-order question is whether these people are more or less than they were before. That question has an answer, but it is not the interesting one.

The more interesting question is: who are they now?

Because here is what role collapse actually produces, underneath the surface disruption of jobs and org charts. It produces an identity crisis that most people will not recognize as one until it has already happened. The title was carrying more of the self-definition than most people realized. When the title stops functioning as a reliable proxy for value, the question that was always there but never urgent becomes suddenly, uncomfortably immediate.

What am I, specifically, that cannot be replicated by someone else with the right tools?

That is not a job question. It is an existential one. And the process, in its slowness, had been deferring it for years.

## Five Questions

What follows is the diagnostic. Five questions an organization can ask to locate itself on the relief-to-panic spectrum. I have written them at the organizational level because that is the level at which most readers will initially receive them. But underneath each organizational question is a personal one. I will name both, because the gap between them is where the real information lives.

### One: When your team reaches a decision, can you describe what changed?

The organizational version: Does your team have a legible decision-making process? Can someone joining the team six months from now reconstruct why you built what you built and not something else?

The personal version: When you were in the last important meeting, did you know what you thought before the meeting ended? Or did you discover what you thought by watching which way the room went?

There is no shame in the second answer. Most meeting culture is designed around it. But if the honest answer is that you routinely leave meetings clearer on the group conclusion than on your own position, that is information. The process was doing some of the thinking.

### Two: What do you do when there is no process to follow?

The organizational version: How does your team behave in genuinely novel situations — problems that fall outside the established workflow? Do people exercise judgment or do they escalate until they find a process that fits?

The personal version: Think of the last time you were handed something with no template, no precedent, and no one to ask. What happened in your body before you started?

Relief means something was activated. Panic means something was exposed. The activation and the exposure were always there. The process was just regulating when and how often you had to feel either one.

### Three: Can you describe your taste?

The organizational version: Does your organization have a point of view that is legible enough that someone outside could describe it? Not your brand guidelines. Not your values statement. Your taste — the thing you consistently choose when both options are defensible.

The personal version: If I asked you to build something without any constraints, any brief, any client requirements — just build the thing you think is most worth building — what would it be? And how quickly did an answer arrive?

Speed of answer is not the point. The point is whether an answer arrived at all, or whether the question felt somehow unanswerable without more information. Taste requires a self. When the process has been providing the constraints, discovering whether you have taste without the process requires the process to be absent.

### Four: Who in your organization slows things down, and why?

The organizational version: Name the people who consistently extend timelines. Now ask honestly: are they slowing things down because they lack capability, or because they are processing something the faster people are not?

The personal version: Are you someone who slows things down? And if so, do you know why?

This is the most uncomfortable question in the five, because the people who are most at risk in the transition to fast execution are often not the ones who know they are at risk. The ones who know they are slow often know why. The ones who do not know — the ones whose process contribution was invisible even to themselves — are the ones most likely to be caught off guard.

### Five: What would you build if you could build anything by tomorrow?

The organizational version: Does your company have a backlog of ideas that have been waiting for execution capacity? When that capacity becomes effectively unlimited, what gets built? Is the answer exciting or terrifying?

The personal version: Do you have something you have been waiting to make?

This is the question that separates the two populations most cleanly. The people who feel relief at cheap execution have an answer ready. They have been carrying it. They have been held back not by lack of ideas but by lack of cycles. When the cycles arrive, something in them accelerates.

The people who feel panic often find that the question itself is the problem. Not the execution. The having-something-to-make. The process was providing the brief. The client was providing the direction. The sprint was providing the container. Without those, the question of what to build next is not liberating. It is vertiginous.

Neither response is a verdict. They are a location. And knowing your location is the beginning of a useful response to it.

## What the Answers Mean

If your honest answers produced mostly relief — if a brief, sharp clarity arrived at each one — you are probably someone whose judgment has been waiting for the process to get out of the way. The risk for you is different: it is the risk of moving so fast, and building so fluidly across traditional role boundaries, that you lose the collaborative texture that made your judgment accurate in the first place. Speed without friction can become speed without correction.

If your answers produced mostly unease — if the questions felt harder than they should, if you found yourself reaching for context that was not in the question — you are probably someone who has been in a productive relationship with process that you have not fully examined. That is not a failure. It is a description. The useful response is not to panic faster, and it is not to dismiss the unease as imposter syndrome. It is to figure out which parts of your current contribution are load-bearing and which parts are borrowed from the structure around you.

Most people will find both in themselves. Relief in some domains. Unease in others. The interesting work is not deciding which camp you are in. It is learning to tell the difference, in real time, between the moment when your judgment is clear and the moment when you are reaching for a process that is no longer there.

## The People Who Will Not See It Coming

There is a specific population this chapter is most worried about.

They are not the people who are clearly going to be displaced — the ones whose entire role was execution of a task that AI now performs faster. Those people know who they are or they will know shortly. The visibility of their situation, while painful, makes it actionable.

The population I am worried about is the ones in the middle. The experienced practitioners whose value has always been described in terms of process knowledge. The person who knows how this organization works, who the stakeholders are, what has been tried before. The person whose contribution is real but difficult to articulate separately from the context they inhabit.

These people are not being replaced. They are being repositioned into a world where their context knowledge is more valuable than ever, but where the way that value shows up has completely changed. They used to show up in the pace and accuracy of execution. They now have to show up in the quality of a decision before execution begins.

That is not a small shift. It is a complete reorientation of where in the process their contribution lands. And most of them do not know it is coming, because nothing in the external description of their role will change until the day it already has.

If you run a small business — a ten-person agency, a regional practice, a firm of two to thirty people — you are this population. The data on small business AI adoption is genuinely conflicting, and the conflict is instructive. Some surveys show adoption jumping sharply year over year. Others show it declining as owners who tried the tools and found them unreliable pulled back. Both experiences are accurate. The tools are genuinely useful and genuinely unreliable in ways that are hard to predict without technical sophistication that most small business owners do not have and should not need.

The honest assessment: the businesses of two to ten employees that are not using AI at all are roughly where they were with cloud software in 2012 — behind, but not yet at the point where the lag is competitively disqualifying. That window is closing. The competitor who figured out which tools actually work for your specific business and integrated them into their operations has a cost advantage and a speed advantage that will compound. But here is the part that matters for this chapter: the small business owner whose competitive advantage was always judgment, relationship, and specialized knowledge — rather than execution capacity — is the one who looks at this moment and sees opportunity rather than threat. The ten-person agency that can now produce what a fifty-person agency used to produce is experiencing relief. The fifty-person agency is the one that should be reading these five questions carefully.

## What You Do With the Answer

If you are reading this and you recognize yourself in the middle — if you are the person who knows more about the organization than you can easily articulate, who has been carrying context for years in a way that has always been valued but never quite made explicit — this is your chapter. Not because it is a warning. Because you are exactly the person whose judgment becomes most important when the process stops doing the distributing.

The question is whether you know that. Whether you can locate it. Whether you can make it legible — to yourself first, and then to the room.

The shift is asking you to externalize something you have always carried internally. That is uncomfortable. It is also the work.

I can write that with some authority because I have done it. Both sides. I was let go from Mastercard in August 2023, after seven years on a product I had worked on passionately. For eighteen months I did not have an identity. I could not find a job — because the box I had been working in had either changed in the seven years I was inside it or I had never fit it to begin with. I experienced relief and panic simultaneously, which I did not know was possible until it happened. What I found, eventually, was the function underneath the title. I am an optimizer and a reframer — the person you bring in to look at a problem and help you work through it. I just didn't know it until the title was gone, and discovering it was not a clean process. Watching what I thought I was disappear was very, very hard. And likely not easier with time.

Most people, if they answer those five questions honestly, will not land cleanly in one column or the other. The relief and the panic will coexist, pointing in different directions at different parts of the same self. The relief pointing at where the judgment was always yours. The panic pointing at where the process was doing more work than you knew.

The audit — what do I know, what have I been carrying, what in my contribution is genuinely mine — that work is yours to do.

*The process was never the point. It was the container. What it was containing is the question.*
